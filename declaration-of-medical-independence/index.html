On entering medicine

I choose medicine because another person’s need gives my learning a purpose, and because the discipline of meeting that need can shape the rest of my life.

I declare these reasons now, while the work of becoming a doctor is still before me. The independence I seek is the freedom to examine what I have been taught and the courage to answer for my judgement. I owe my colleagues reasons they can test. I owe a patient an explanation they can understand, the chance to correct me and the freedom to choose differently.

What I owe another person

Altruism gives me a question to carry into the room. Whose need am I serving when I speak now? I want to learn empathy well enough to set aside a clever question when someone needs a moment to find their own words. That takes self-awareness. My wish to reassure someone can be a way of escaping my own discomfort with their distress.

I want to learn to read the social life of a consultation. Who gets interrupted? Whose account becomes the official one? If a relative answers every question, I can make room for the patient’s voice. If an examination recalls a past trauma, I can explain what I propose, ask what would help them feel safe and honour a request to pause. A patient can understand me perfectly and still decline. Helping someone heal begins with taking their authority over their own experience seriously.

A discipline for life

I want six years of medical study to change what I do by instinct. My analogy is special forces training. What I want to carry over is the rehearsal before a difficult task, the ability to orient myself quickly when a plan fails and the honesty of the debrief afterward. In medicine, that discipline matters most when it gives me the composure to listen, call for help or change my mind in time.

The reasoning buildings I want to construct

I want to master the different forms of reasoning and learn how to build with them. I think of a “reasoning building” as a structure I can enter when a problem is unfamiliar. One room is for generating explanations. Another is for asking what each explanation predicts. A third is for weighing what happened. The doors matter as much as the rooms. If a prediction fails, can I find my way back to the assumption that produced it?

Suppose two accounts of an event disagree. Abduction helps me propose ways both people could have reported honestly. Deduction makes me say what else I would expect to find if each account were right. Bayesian reasoning asks how far new evidence should move my confidence. Looking back, I can ask which question actually helped and which merely made my first hunch sound more sophisticated. I want to practise that movement until I can recognise when a different kind of question is called for.

I want the habit to travel with me. In an argument with someone I love, I might recognise how quickly I have mistaken my first account for the whole story. I can ask what I missed, hear the answer and repair what I did. The speed I value is the time from realising I was wrong to doing something wiser.

What existentialism asks of my learning

No qualification can decide for me what kind of person I will become. I make that choice in ordinary acts, including the times I return to a mistake I could quietly forget. If I say I value courage, I can look for the occasion on which I admitted uncertainty to someone whose opinion mattered to me. That is how I want to turn a chosen value into a lived one.

Feedback and consciousness

To model a feedback loop in conscious life, I can start with a single episode. What caught my attention? What meaning did I give it? How did I act, and what did that action make easier or harder for me to notice next time? Here is one loop I want to learn to change.

  1. Feedback loops

    Suppose I take silence for agreement. I move on, leaving the other person less opportunity to speak, and their continued silence seems to confirm my reading. My own action helped produce the evidence I trusted.

  2. Adaptability

    In a practice conversation, I can write down what I believe we agreed on, invite my partner to describe it in their own words and compare our accounts. If they understood and simply disagree, the change belongs in my interpretation. If my explanation was unclear, I can try another and ask again. This is a small piece of conscious life I can deliberately redesign and test.

  3. Lock-in

    Repetition can entrench the mistake or secure the habit that catches it. I want the pause before assuming agreement to become automatic. I also want an exit from my own routine. If my partner tells me that my checking feels like an interrogation, their experience gives me a reason to change how I ask. The habit worth keeping is my willingness to hear the answer.

My undertaking

I make these commitments while I am still learning what medicine will ask of me. When I add to this declaration, I want to be able to say what happened, whose words stayed with me and what I have done differently since.
